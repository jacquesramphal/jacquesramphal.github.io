# Taste as the deliverable

_About ten minutes, then a triage session on a pile of generated UI. Cut what doesn't belong, and write the because for each cut._

This is the chapter the whole course was walking toward. Everything before it, reading code, owning tokens, directing agents, was clearing away the work that's becoming cheap so you can spend your attention on the work that isn't. This is that work. Deciding what's good, and being able to say why, is the thing you're paid for now.

Here's the situation you'll actually be in. An agent, or three agents, hands you twenty versions of a screen. All of them render. Most of them are fine. Your job is not to admire them, and it's not to ship the first one that loads. Your job is to find the one that's right and remove everything keeping it from being right.

That's triage: deciding where your attention goes when everything in front of you is competing for it and most of it doesn't deserve it.

## A drift audit, out loud

Take a pile of generated UI and walk it looking for the tells. There's a frame sitting a few pixels off the grid, the agent splitting a difference again. There's a third gray that nobody chose, hiding between the two grays that were decided. There's one element too many: a badge, a second CTA, a helper line explaining something the design should have made obvious.

None of these are bugs. Everything works. That's what makes them the hard cut, because you're not removing what's broken, you're removing what's merely allowed. The off-grid frame gets pulled to the grid. The third gray collapses into the nearest real one. The one thing too many comes off.

Each cut needs a because, said in one line. "Removed the second CTA: two primary actions means no primary action." The because is the deliverable. The clean screen is just what's left after you've defended every removal.

## Your turn

Generate a screen, or take one you already have a pile of versions of. Run the audit. Find the off-grid element, the extra gray, the one thing too many. Cut them.

For every cut, write the because in a single sentence. If you can't write the because, you haven't found the real reason to cut it yet, and you either find it or leave the thing alone. A cut you can't defend is taste you're asserting instead of taste you're using.

The oldest version of this rule is the one that still works best. Before you leave the house, take one thing off.
