## Pressure-test

### Will anyone pay? (gut-check)
Yes, but a narrower slice than "anxious designers," and demand isn't the risk. Reskilling-under-threat is one of the most reliable "people pay" motivations there is. Three qualifiers:
1. **The scared and the capable are different people.** The most terrified (junior/mid) have the least money and the least chance of actually making the leap (it needs coding aptitude a course can't install). The real buyer is mid-to-senior designers with a technical streak.
2. **Saturated by launch.** Everyone's shipping an "AI for designers" course. The only defense is the thing almost no seller can fake: you've built production agent systems and shipped design systems in real code. Positioning must make that unmistakable.
3. **Honest promise.** A course can't make someone a senior engineer. Sell the workflow, tooling, mental models, and taste framework — not a transformation you can't deliver. Overpromising torches the reputation that is the whole asset.

The risk was never demand. It's the same one as everything else: no audience to sell to, and this is the slowest-to-money of the good ideas.

### Who (pick one)
- **Primary:** mid-to-senior product/UX designers with a technical streak who want to move toward design engineering. Employed (money), motivated (career), capable (aptitude).
- **Premium adjacent (later):** design leaders who need to understand AX to lead — different, higher-priced, strategy-flavored product.
- **Avoid as primary:** terrified juniors (can't afford it, can't do it) and engineers (not the natural audience).

### Positioning / wedge
Not "AI for designers" (commodity). **"The design engineer path — become the person who directs the machine, not the one it replaces."** Proof is the moat: Genie, token pipelines, production code, the writing. Taste is Triage is the intellectual spine.

### Curriculum
1. **The new shape of the job** — bifurcation, taste-as-triage, what "design engineer" means, honest "is this you?" filter.
2. **Code without becoming an engineer** — front-of-the-front-end, reading/steering AI-generated code, code-friction-as-discipline. *Hands-on: ship one real component with an agent.*
3. **Tokens as the contract** — architecture, theming, Figma↔code sync. *Hands-on: build a small token system.*
4. **Directing agents** — the /design pattern, `.cursorrules`, guardrails, diff-based handoff. *Hands-on: stand up an agent workflow.*
5. **Taste as the deliverable** — judgment frameworks, catching drift, being the human gate, saying "why."
6. **Repositioning yourself** — portfolio-as-evidence, record-not-portfolio, being findable, interviewing for the resilient tier.
- **Capstone:** ship a small design-system-with-agents project. Students' results become the testimonials.

### Format & price
Don't build a big recorded course into the void. Sequence:
1. **One small live cohort, premium (~$1,000–1,500), 5–6 weeks.** Validates with real money, generates testimonials, refines the material.
2. **Then productize the recording** as an evergreen self-paced tier (~$300–500), cohort as the premium tier on top.
Underpricing is the mistake, not overpricing. A $49 course signals junk and attracts refund-prone buyers.

### Launch mechanics (the deciding part)
A cohort needs an audience that doesn't exist yet. So the launch path is an *audience* path:
1. **The publication plan is the top-of-funnel.** Ship the on-thesis essays, do the talks, post consistently. #4 and the publication plan are the same project.
2. **Capture an email list** (lead magnet: token starter / Design Guard → "design engineer starter kit").
3. **Pre-sell before building.** Waitlist → sell the cohort with a deposit before recording anything. Fewer than ~10 paid holds → don't build.
4. **Borrow an audience for run one** — co-teach/guest in an existing community, or run the pilot into the warm MACH network.
Honest timeline: **6–12 months** (audience → list → pre-sell → cohort). Highest ceiling, slowest money. Fund life with contracting/leverage-in-place while it compounds.

### Failure modes (ranked)
1. **Empty cohort (no audience)** — the killer. Pre-sell; borrow an audience; never build first.
2. **Saturation** — beaten by credibility + "design engineer, not AI-for-designers."
3. **Time** — live cohorts are heavy, off-hours. Keep run one small.
4. **Refunds/credibility** — honest promise; screen for aptitude.
5. **Orium/visibility** — education is less conflicting than a competing studio, but confirm moonlighting terms and mind the time.
6. **Expert ≠ teacher** — run one will be rough; keep it small and cheap.

### Minimum viable test (before building)
Don't build a course. Cheapest proof:
- Publish 2–3 on-thesis pieces + post for ~2–3 months.
- Stand up a one-page cohort landing + waitlist.
- **Run one paid pilot workshop — half-day, ~$200–300, 8–12 seats — into the warm MACH network first.** Can't fill a cheap warm workshop → the course won't fly. Fill it → validation + testimonials + content.

### Verdict
Best non-job bet, with one reframe: success is ~80% distribution (the publishing flywheel), ~20% the course. First move is the publication plan + a warm pilot, not curriculum. Sequence it, kill-switch at each stage (no pre-sales → stop), fund life elsewhere meanwhile.

### Hero
**Become the designer AI can't replace.**

The execution layer of design is being automated. The designers who thrive aren't the fastest in Figma — they're the ones who can direct the machine, ship in real code, and say why a decision is right. This is a cohort for designers who want to become that person.

`[Join the waitlist →]`

### The situation
AI made design output nearly free. A layout, a component library, a full page — generated in minutes. That's not the threat. The threat is that when making is cheap, the people who only make get commoditized, and the people who judge, direct, and ship move up. That gap is widening every month.

You don't need to become a software engineer. You need to work in code enough to steer it, own a token system, direct agents instead of fighting them, and keep taste as the thing you're paid for.

### Who this is for
- Mid-to-senior product or UX designers who can already think in systems.
- You've touched code or want to, and you're ready to work in it, not just talk about it.
- You want to move toward design engineering and stay relevant as the tools get smarter.

### Who this is not for
- If you've never opened a code editor and don't want to start, this isn't the one.
- If you want AI to make prettier pictures faster, there are cheaper courses for that.
- This won't make you a senior engineer in six weeks. It will change how you work and what you're worth.

### What you'll build
Over [six weeks], hands-on, you'll:
- Ship a real component in code with an agent, and learn to read and steer what it writes.
- Build a small design-token system that keeps Figma and code in sync.
- Set up an agent workflow with guardrails and diff-based handoff.
- Practice the judgment part — catching drift, being the human gate, saying why.
- Rebuild how you present yourself for the roles that are actually growing.
- **Capstone:** ship a small design-system-with-agents project you can show.

### Who's teaching
I'm Jacques Ramphal. I've spent [13] years at the seam between design and engineering — designing token-based systems and writing the production code that ships them. I built Genie, an agentic delivery platform a team uses daily, and I write about where this craft is going. I'm teaching this because the path I took by accident is the one a lot of designers now need on purpose.
